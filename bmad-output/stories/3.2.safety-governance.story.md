## Dev Notes

<!-- LOCKED. External dev tools must not edit Dev Notes. -->

### Purpose

This content serves as Phase 3 (partial) of the restructured Lesson 17, establishing AI safety and governance as a conceptual frame for the entire four-part series. Students must understand safety concerns before building AI+FHIR applications in the downstream lessons. [Source: SPEC.md#problem, tech-spec.md#problem-statement]

### Topics (from tech-spec.md#FR-010)

1. **Hallucination risks in clinical contexts**: Define hallucination as confident but fabricated output. Provide concrete examples:
   - LLM invents a medication the patient never took (appears in summary but not in FHIR MedicationRequest resources)
   - LLM fabricates a lab value (e.g., "A1c: 8.2" when no such Observation exists)
   - LLM conflates two patients' data (particularly risky in batch processing)

   [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010; examples are inference from clinical safety principles]

2. **Why local models matter for PHI (HIPAA considerations)**: Sending patient data to a cloud LLM API (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic) without a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) is a HIPAA violation. Local models (Ollama, LM Studio) keep data on-premises. Concrete scenario: "If you copy a patient's FHIR bundle and paste it into ChatGPT to summarize, you've just disclosed PHI." [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010, tech-spec.md#security]

3. **Human-in-the-loop: AI as decision support, not decision maker**: The LLM suggests or summarizes; the clinician validates and decides. Example: Exercise 3 produces a patient summary, but the clinician must verify it against the source FHIR data before using it in care decisions. [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010]

4. **The "golden rule": never trust LLM output without validation**: All LLM output must be validated before use. Validation strategies:
   - Cross-check against source FHIR data (does the summary match the actual resources?)
   - Structured output validation (does the LLM output conform to the expected schema?)
   - Human review (clinician spot-checks critical facts)

   This is the single most important safety principle for the series. [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010]

5. **Regulatory awareness (ONC HTI-1, FDA AI devices)**: Brief mention at awareness level:
   - **ONC HTI-1**: Interoperability standards that include decision support and predictive models; students should know this framework exists and shapes health IT policy
   - **FDA AI/ML as medical devices**: If an AI tool is used for diagnosis or treatment decisions, it may be regulated as a medical device; students should understand that not all AI+FHIR applications are unregulated

   Do NOT dive into compliance requirements, approval pathways, or legal details — the goal is awareness, not expertise. [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010]

### Forward References to Part One (AC: #3)

Content must frame safety as a series-long theme. When discussing validation and human-in-the-loop, forward-reference safety patterns covered in Part One:

- **Least privilege**: Agents are granted minimal FHIR access scope (e.g., read-only, specific resource types). Covered in Part One's agent architecture section.
- **Structured validation**: Agents produce structured output (JSON with schemas) that can be validated programmatically. Covered in Part One's tool-use patterns.
- **Audit logging**: All agent actions are logged for review. Covered in Part One's safety patterns section.

These forward references show students that the safety principles introduced here are operationalized in the later lessons. [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010]

### Content Format

Deliverable is a markdown document or slide outline. Visual formatting (fonts, colors, diagrams) is out of scope — instructor will format according to preference. Focus on textual content and inline notes for suggested visuals (e.g., "Diagram: data flow from FHIR server to local LLM vs. cloud LLM with BAA checkpoint"). [Source: tech-spec.md#FR-010]

### Tone and Framing

Safety content should be serious but not alarmist. The message is: "AI is a powerful tool for healthcare, AND we must use it responsibly." Avoid framing LLMs as inherently dangerous or untrustworthy; instead, frame safety as a design principle (like sterile technique in surgery — necessary, but doesn't mean surgery is unsafe). [Inference]

### Series Context

This content is part of Phase 3 (discussion/bridge) of Lesson 17, which is the first lesson in a four-part series:
1. Lesson 17 — foundations (AI concepts, exercises, safety frame)
2. Part One — agentic AI architecture, MCP, tool-use, safety patterns
3. Part Two — FHIR as knowledge layer, subagents, safety operationalization
4. RAG Lab — vector stores, retrieval, safety in RAG pipelines

Safety is introduced here and deepened in each subsequent lesson. [Source: SPEC.md#series-context, tech-spec.md#problem-statement]

## Testing

### Strategy

Content testing is primarily pedagogical review. The dev tool should verify:

1. **Completeness check**: Confirm all 5 required topics are present in the content file (hallucination risks, PHI/HIPAA, human-in-the-loop, golden rule, regulatory awareness). This is a manual checklist against tech-spec.md#FR-010.

2. **Scenario audit**: For each topic, verify at least one concrete FHIR+AI scenario is present (e.g., "LLM invents a medication" for hallucination, "copying bundle to ChatGPT" for PHI risk). Mark any topic lacking a concrete scenario.

3. **Forward reference check**: Verify that Part One safety patterns (least privilege, structured validation, audit logging) are mentioned as forward references (not taught in depth here). Search for these terms and confirm they are framed as "covered in Part One."

4. **Tone review**: Read the content and flag any language that is alarmist or dismissive of LLM utility. Safety content should be balanced: acknowledge risks, teach mitigation, and frame AI as a tool that requires responsible use.

5. **Regulatory depth check**: Confirm ONC HTI-1 and FDA AI/ML are mentioned at awareness level only. If the content explains approval pathways, compliance requirements, or legal standards in detail, it has gone too deep — mark for simplification.

No test code, no coverage metrics, no automated fixtures. This is human-reviewed content.
