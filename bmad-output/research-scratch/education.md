## 1. Foundational AI Concepts for Healthcare Audiences

### 1.1 Core Concepts Healthcare Informatics Students Need

Based on research across multiple sources, the following AI/LLM concepts form the essential knowledge base for healthcare informatics students. They are grouped by priority tier.

**Tier 1 -- Must Cover (Core Literacy)**

- **What LLMs are and how they generate text**: LLMs are "giant statistical prediction machines that repeatedly predict the next word in a sequence" (IBM, "What Are Large Language Models?"). Students need to understand that these models do not "think" or "understand" -- they compute probabilities over sequences of tokens.
  - Source: IBM, "What Are Large Language Models?", https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/large-language-models, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Tokenization**: The process of breaking text into smaller units. "Most modern language models tokenize by subwords, that is, by chunks of text containing semantic meaning." For English, approximately one token equals ~4 characters or about 3/4 of a word. Example: "unwatched" breaks into three subwords: "un" (prefix), "watch" (root), and "ed" (suffix).
  - Source: Google Developers, "Introduction to Large Language Models", https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/resources/intro-llms, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Context windows**: The "maximum number of tokens that a model can 'see' and use at once when generating text" (IBM). This is critical for healthcare because patient records can be large, and understanding context window limits directly affects how FHIR data can be processed.
  - Source: IBM, "What Are Large Language Models?", https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/large-language-models, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Prompt engineering**: The practice of crafting inputs to guide LLM outputs. Key techniques include:
  - Using structured context organization (identity, instructions, examples, context)
  - Few-shot learning: demonstrating desired behavior through sample input-output pairs
  - Developer vs. user message roles for establishing authority hierarchy
  - Source: OpenAI, "Prompt Engineering Guide", https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Temperature**: Controls "the randomness of text that is generated by LLMs during inference" (IBM). Low temperature produces more deterministic, focused output; high temperature produces more creative, varied output. For clinical applications, low temperature is generally preferred for accuracy.
  - Source: IBM, "What Are Large Language Models?", https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/large-language-models, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Hallucination/confabulation**: LLMs can generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information. This is the single most important safety concept for healthcare audiences, as clinical decisions based on hallucinated information could harm patients.

**Tier 2 -- Should Cover (Deeper Understanding)**

- **Embeddings**: Numerical vector representations of tokens. The model learns to position related concepts nearby in vector space -- for instance, "dog" and "bark" appear closer together than "bark" and "tree" when analyzing dog-related text (IBM).
  - Source: IBM, "What Are Large Language Models?", https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/large-language-models, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Transformers and self-attention**: The transformer architecture uses a self-attention mechanism allowing models to "pay attention to" different tokens selectively. The self-attention mechanism "assigns a weight to each part of the input data while processing it," determining which elements deserve focus (NVIDIA). Together with positional encoding, this enables analysis of "subtle ways and contexts in which distinct elements influence and relate to each other over long distances."
  - Source: NVIDIA, "Large Language Models", https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/glossary/large-language-models/, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**: A technique that "optimizing the output of a large language model, so it references an authoritative knowledge base outside of its training data sources before generating a response" (AWS). RAG involves: (1) loading documents, (2) chunking them into segments, (3) creating embeddings, (4) storing in vector databases, and (5) retrieving relevant chunks at query time.
  - Source: AWS, "What is RAG?", https://aws.amazon.com/what-is/retrieval-augmented-generation/, accessed 2026-10-07.
  - Source: Anthropic, "Contextual Retrieval", https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval, accessed 2026-10-07.
  - Source: LangChain, "Tutorial: ChatGPT Over Your Data", https://www.langchain.com/blog/tutorial-chatgpt-over-your-data, accessed 2026-10-07.

- **Fine-tuning**: Customizing pre-trained models on domain-specific data. Rather than retraining from scratch, enterprises use "prompt tuning, fine-tuning, and adapters" to customize foundation models for specific applications (NVIDIA).
  - Source: NVIDIA, "Large Language Models", https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/glossary/large-language-models/, accessed 2026-10-07.

**Tier 3 -- Brief Mention (Awareness Level)**

- **AI agents**: Systems that use LLMs to plan and execute multi-step tasks autonomously, often with tool use capabilities.
- **Model architectures**: Encoder-only (BERT, for classification), decoder-only (GPT, for generation), encoder-decoder (T5, for translation/summarization) (NVIDIA).
- **Training data and bias**: How training data composition affects model outputs and potential disparities in healthcare applications.
- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**: How models are aligned with human preferences after pre-training.

### 1.2 Explaining Concepts Without CS/ML Background

**Key pedagogical principles for non-CS audiences:**

1. **Lead with function, not mechanism**: Start with what the technology does and why it matters for healthcare, then selectively peel back layers of how it works. Healthcare professionals are accustomed to using tools they understand at a functional level (e.g., MRI physics vs. reading MRI images).

2. **Use healthcare-native analogies** (see Section 1.3 below).

3. **Anchor to concrete examples**: Every concept should be demonstrated with a healthcare-specific example, ideally one involving FHIR data.

4. **Avoid mathematical notation**: Transformers, attention, and embeddings can all be explained conceptually without matrices or equations.

5. **Emphasize the "why it matters for you" framing**: For each concept, explicitly connect to how it affects the informaticist's daily work or the systems they build.

### 1.3 Best Analogies and Teaching Approaches for Non-CS Audiences

**Tokenization:**
- Analogy: "Like a pharmacist reading a prescription -- they don't read letter by letter, they recognize meaningful chunks: drug names, dosages, routes. LLMs break text into similar meaningful chunks called tokens."
- Hands-on: Show students how a tokenizer breaks clinical text (use tiktoken or a web-based tokenizer tool to visualize).

**Embeddings:**
- Analogy: "Imagine a hospital map where departments are arranged by relatedness -- cardiology near cardiac surgery, orthopedics near physical therapy. Embeddings are like a multi-dimensional map where words with similar meanings are placed near each other."
- Analogy (alternative): "Like ICD codes -- codes that are numerically close often represent related conditions. Embeddings do this automatically for all language."

**Context Window:**
- Analogy: "The context window is like a clinician's working memory during a patient encounter. A doctor can only hold so much information in mind at once. If the patient's chart is 500 pages, you can't read it all simultaneously -- you have to be selective about what's in front of you."
- Practical: "A 128K token context window is roughly 96,000 words or ~300 pages. A typical FHIR patient bundle might be 2,000-10,000 tokens, so one patient fits easily, but a population-level analysis might not."

**Attention/Self-Attention:**
- Analogy: "When a radiologist reads a chest X-ray, they selectively attend to relevant structures. Self-attention is similar -- the model learns which words in a sentence are most relevant to understanding each other word."
- Analogy (alternative): "Like a differential diagnosis -- a clinician weighing which symptoms are most relevant to which possible conditions."

**Temperature:**
- Analogy: "Temperature is like the difference between a structured clinical protocol (low temperature = predictable, consistent output) and a brainstorming session (high temperature = more creative, diverse, but less predictable output)."
- Practical: "For patient summaries, use low temperature (0.0-0.3). For generating patient education materials, slightly higher (0.5-0.7) might be acceptable."

**RAG:**
- Analogy: "RAG is like an open-book exam versus a closed-book exam. Without RAG, the model relies only on what it memorized during training. With RAG, it can look up current, authoritative information before answering."
- Healthcare analogy: "Like a clinician consulting UpToDate or clinical guidelines while seeing a patient, rather than relying solely on what they remember from medical school."

**Hallucination:**
- Analogy: "Like confabulation in neurology -- the patient isn't lying, their brain is confidently filling in gaps with plausible but incorrect information. LLMs do the same thing."
- Critical framing: "This is why you never trust an LLM's clinical output without verification, just as you verify drug interactions or lab reference ranges."

**Prompt Engineering:**
- Analogy: "Like writing a good consultation request -- the more specific and structured your question, the better the response. 'What do you think about this patient?' gets a vague answer. 'Given this 45-year-old diabetic with rising creatinine, please assess renal function and recommend next steps' gets a focused one."

### 1.4 Recommended Depth for a Single Lesson

For a single lesson within a FHIR training course (estimated 2-3 hours), the recommended approach based on educational research is:

**Cover in depth (with hands-on exercises):**
- What LLMs are and how they generate text (conceptual)
- Tokenization (brief demo with tokenizer)
- Context windows (demonstrated through chunking FHIR data)
- Prompt engineering (hands-on: writing prompts for clinical tasks)
- Temperature (demonstrated through side-by-side comparisons)
- Hallucination and safety (critical discussion)
- RAG (conceptual, with connection to FHIR data)

**Cover conceptually (brief explanation, no exercises):**
- Embeddings
- Transformers/attention (high-level only)
- Fine-tuning vs. RAG tradeoffs

**Mention briefly (awareness only):**
- AI agents
- Model architectures
- Training data, RLHF
- Specific model comparisons (GPT vs. Claude vs. Llama etc.)

### 2.2 Curriculum Framework Recommendations

Based on the published literature and organizational guidance, the following framework emerges:

**Miller's Pyramid Applied to AI Competency (Houser et al., 2026):**

| Level | AI Competency | Example Activities |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Knows** | Define AI/ML concepts, explain limitations | Lecture, readings, concept quizzes |
| **Knows How** | Describe when and how AI applies to informatics | Case studies, scenario analysis |
| **Shows How** | Demonstrate AI tool use in supervised setting | Guided exercises, live demos |
| **Does** | Independently apply AI tools in practice | Projects, real-world application |

**Recommended Competency Domains for Health Informatics Students:**

1. **Foundational AI Literacy**: Understanding what AI is, types of AI, how LLMs work at a conceptual level
2. **Clinical AI Applications**: Where AI is used in healthcare (diagnosis, imaging, NLP, CDS, summarization)
3. **Data and Interoperability**: How AI systems consume healthcare data, role of standards like FHIR
4. **Prompt Engineering and Tool Use**: Practical skills in using AI tools effectively
5. **Ethics, Bias, and Safety**: Understanding risks, bias in training data, hallucination, regulatory landscape
6. **Evaluation and Validation**: How to assess AI system outputs for clinical appropriateness

### 2.3 Hands-on Exercises That Work Best

Based on pedagogical research and the existing lesson's approach, the most effective exercises for clinicians/informaticists are:

1. **Prompt engineering workshops**: Students write and iterate on prompts for clinical tasks, comparing outputs. This is consistently rated as the most engaging exercise because it requires no programming background and produces immediately visible results.

2. **Side-by-side comparisons**: Students compare LLM outputs at different temperatures, with different prompts, or from different models on the same clinical data. This builds critical evaluation skills.

3. **Structured data interpretation**: Students give an LLM structured FHIR data and evaluate whether the model correctly interprets clinical codes, values, and relationships.

4. **Error detection exercises**: Students are given LLM outputs that contain hallucinations or errors, and must identify them. This builds the critical skill of not blindly trusting AI output.

5. **Live demonstrations with local models**: The existing lesson's approach of using LM Studio with local models (Llama) is excellent because it:
   - Eliminates data privacy concerns
   - Allows experimentation without API costs
   - Demonstrates that AI can run locally
   - Makes concepts like context windows tangible

### 2.4 Common Misconceptions About AI Among Healthcare Professionals

Based on the literature and clinical AI deployment experience:

1. **"AI understands the patient"**: LLMs process statistical patterns, not meaning. They do not understand pathophysiology, patient preferences, or clinical context the way a clinician does.

2. **"AI is always right / AI is always wrong"**: Both extremes are harmful. AI outputs need verification but are often useful.

3. **"AI will replace clinicians"**: Research consistently shows AI is most effective as augmentation, not replacement. The AMA deliberately uses the term "Augmented Intelligence" for this reason.

4. **"More data always means better AI"**: Data quality, representativeness, and relevance matter more than volume.

5. **"AI is a black box we can never understand"**: While deep learning models are complex, techniques like attention visualization, prompt engineering, and RAG provide meaningful interpretability.

6. **"If it's trained on medical data, it's safe for clinical use"**: Training data quality, regulatory approval (FDA/CE marking for SaMD), clinical validation, and workflow integration all matter.

7. **"Prompt engineering is trivial"**: Crafting effective prompts for clinical tasks requires domain expertise, understanding of model behavior, and iterative refinement.

8. **"AI hallucination is a bug that will be fixed"**: Hallucination is an inherent property of probabilistic text generation, not a simple bug. It can be mitigated (through RAG, grounding, structured prompts) but not eliminated.

## 3. Practical Exercises Combining AI with FHIR

### 3.1 Patient Record Summarization (Existing Exercise)

**What the current lesson does:** The existing `fhirapp.py` demonstrates a multi-stage summarization pipeline:
1. Retrieves all FHIR resources for a patient using `$everything` operation
2. Uses `tiktoken` to chunk the data into 1,500-token segments
3. Summarizes each chunk individually using a local LLM (Llama 3.2 3B)
4. Merges batch summaries into intermediate summaries (batches of 4)
5. Produces a final patient summary from intermediate summaries
6. Displays results in a Textual (terminal UI) application with progress tracking

**Pedagogical value:** HIGH. This exercise demonstrates:
- Context window limitations (why chunking is needed)
- Tokenization (using tiktoken directly)
- Prompt engineering (the summarization prompts)
- Multi-step AI reasoning (the map-reduce summarization pattern)
- FHIR data consumption ($everything operation)
- Practical constraints (local model size, token limits)

**Suggested enhancements:**
- Add a step where students modify the prompt and compare outputs
- Add temperature comparison (run same prompt at temp 0.0 vs 0.7)
- Add an exercise where students identify errors in the generated summary
- Discuss what happens when important data is split across chunks

### 3.2 Natural Language to FHIR Query Translation

**Exercise concept:** Students ask clinical questions in natural language and the LLM translates them into valid FHIR search API calls.

**Example interactions:**
- Student: "Find all patients with diabetes diagnosed in the last year"
- LLM output: `GET /Condition?code=http://snomed.info/sct|73211009&onset-date=ge2025-10-07`
- Student: "Show me the latest hemoglobin A1c for patient John Smith"
- LLM output: `GET /Observation?patient.name=Smith,John&code=http://loinc.org|4548-4&_sort=-date&_count=1`

**Implementation approach:**
- Provide the LLM with a system prompt containing FHIR search parameter documentation
- Students write natural language queries
- LLM generates FHIR API URLs
- Students execute the generated URLs against the local FHIR server
- Students validate whether the query returns correct results

**Pedagogical value:** HIGH. Reinforces both FHIR search syntax and AI capabilities/limitations. Students see firsthand when the LLM generates invalid search parameters or uses wrong code systems.

**Connection to FHIR curriculum:** Directly leverages knowledge from earlier lessons on FHIR search. The existing `getSearchPatients.py` already demonstrates FHIR search infrastructure.

### 3.3 Clinical Note Generation from FHIR Data

**Exercise concept:** Students provide structured FHIR resources (Patient, Conditions, Observations, MedicationRequests) and the LLM generates a clinical narrative.

**Example:**
- Input: FHIR Patient resource + recent Conditions + Observations
- Output: "Mrs. Jane Doe is a 67-year-old female with a history of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (diagnosed 2019), hypertension, and chronic kidney disease stage 3. Her most recent HbA1c was 7.8% (2026-09-15), up from 7.2% three months prior..."

**Pedagogical value:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Demonstrates:
- How structured data maps to clinical narratives
- The narrative element in FHIR resources (and how AI could generate it)
- Potential for errors when LLM misinterprets FHIR coding
- The value of human review of AI-generated clinical text

### 3.4 AI-Assisted Clinical Decision Support Using FHIR Resources

**Exercise concept:** Students build a simple CDS pipeline:
1. Retrieve patient data via FHIR
2. Feed data to LLM with a clinical decision support prompt
3. LLM identifies potential issues (drug interactions, missing screenings, abnormal trends)

**Connection to CDS Hooks:** The CDS Hooks standard (HL7, v2.0.1 STU 2) provides the framework for integrating CDS into clinical workflows. CDS Hooks uses FHIR as its data model and supports "prefetch templates" where "services declare FHIR queries needed to compute recommendations." Services should "respond quickly (on the order of 500 ms)" -- an important real-world constraint to discuss.
- Source: CDS Hooks, https://cds-hooks.hl7.org/, accessed 2026-10-07.

**Pedagogical value:** MEDIUM. More conceptual than hands-on at a single-lesson scale, but valuable for understanding the CDS ecosystem. Could be simplified to: "Given this patient's data, what alerts would you expect a CDS system to generate?" and then comparing human answers with LLM answers.

### 3.5 Code Generation for FHIR Operations Using AI

**Exercise concept:** Students use an LLM to generate Python code for FHIR operations:
- "Write a Python function that retrieves all Observations for a patient and filters for abnormal lab values"
- "Generate code to create a new FHIR Encounter resource and POST it to the server"

**Pedagogical value:** MEDIUM. Best suited for students with some programming background. Demonstrates AI as a coding assistant and allows students to evaluate whether generated code follows FHIR conventions correctly.

### 3.6 Ranking of Exercises by Pedagogical Effectiveness

For a single lesson in a FHIR training course, exercises ranked by effectiveness:

| Rank | Exercise | Why | Prerequisites |
|------|----------|-----|---------------|
| 1 | Patient Record Summarization (enhanced) | Tangible, demonstrates multiple concepts, already implemented | Basic Python, FHIR basics |
| 2 | Natural Language to FHIR Query Translation | Reinforces FHIR search, reveals AI limitations | FHIR search knowledge |
| 3 | Clinical Note Generation | Connects structured and narrative data | FHIR resource understanding |
| 4 | Error Detection / Hallucination Hunting | Builds critical evaluation skills | AI output to review |
| 5 | AI-Assisted CDS | Shows real-world application | CDS concepts |
| 6 | Code Generation for FHIR | Useful but requires programming | Python proficiency |

## 4. Lesson Structure Recommendations

### 4.1 Optimal Sequencing

Based on educational design research, the recommended approach for a single AI+FHIR lesson is a **"Sandwich" model**: Demo first, then concepts, then hands-on practice.

**Recommended sequence (2.5-3 hour lesson):**

**Phase 1: Hook and Motivation (15 min)**
- Live demonstration: Run the existing FHIR summarization app
- Show input (raw FHIR JSON) alongside output (patient summary)
- Pose the question: "How did the computer do this? What could go wrong?"

**Phase 2: Foundational Concepts (45 min)**
- What are LLMs? (5 min) -- statistical next-token prediction
- Tokenization (5 min) -- live demo with tokenizer
- Context windows (5 min) -- why the app chunks data
- Prompt engineering (10 min) -- anatomy of a good prompt
- Temperature and sampling (5 min) -- side-by-side comparison
- Hallucination and safety (10 min) -- critical for healthcare
- RAG overview (5 min) -- connecting AI to authoritative data

**Phase 3: Guided Hands-on (60 min)**
- Exercise 1 (20 min): Modify prompts in the summarization app, compare outputs
- Exercise 2 (20 min): Natural language to FHIR query translation
- Exercise 3 (20 min): Clinical note generation OR error detection exercise

**Phase 4: Advanced Concepts and Discussion (20 min)**
- Embeddings, transformers, attention (conceptual overview)
- Fine-tuning vs. RAG tradeoffs
- AI agents and tool use
- The regulatory landscape for clinical AI

**Phase 5: Wrap-up and Resources (10 min)**
- Key takeaways
- Resource list for further learning
- Brief assessment or reflection exercise

### 4.2 Theory vs. Practice Balance

For a single lesson aimed at healthcare informatics students:

- **Target ratio: 40% concepts / 60% hands-on**
- Lead with a demonstration to establish motivation
- Interleave short concept explanations with immediate practical application
- Avoid long lecture blocks (>15 min without interaction)
- Every concept should connect to a concrete healthcare or FHIR example

**Rationale:** Healthcare informatics students are typically practice-oriented professionals. They learn best when they can immediately see how concepts apply to their work. Pure theory without application loses engagement quickly. However, some conceptual foundation is essential to prevent "magical thinking" about AI capabilities.

### 4.3 Assessment Approaches for AI Literacy

**Formative (during lesson):**
- Prompt engineering challenge: "Write a prompt that generates a discharge summary from this FHIR data. Compare with your neighbor's prompt."
- Error detection: "Here is an AI-generated patient summary. Find three errors."
- Concept check questions: "Why did the app need to chunk the patient data?" "What happens if you increase the temperature to 1.0?"

**Summative (post-lesson):**
- Short quiz on key concepts (tokenization, context windows, hallucination, RAG)
- Practical task: Given a new FHIR dataset and a clinical question, write an appropriate prompt and evaluate the AI output
- Reflection essay: "Describe one way AI/LLMs could improve and one way they could harm clinical informatics workflows in your organization"

**Bloom's Taxonomy Alignment:**
- Remember: Define tokenization, context window, hallucination
- Understand: Explain why chunking is necessary for large FHIR bundles
- Apply: Write a prompt to summarize patient data
- Analyze: Compare outputs from different prompts or temperatures
- Evaluate: Assess AI-generated clinical content for accuracy and safety
- Create: Design an AI-assisted workflow for a clinical informatics use case

## 5. Analysis of the Existing Lesson

### 5.1 Current Strengths

The existing Lesson 17 code demonstrates several best practices:

1. **Uses local LLM (LM Studio + Llama)**: No cloud dependency, no data privacy concerns, demonstrates that AI can run locally.
2. **Shows real FHIR integration**: Uses $everything operation, real FHIR resource parsing.
3. **Demonstrates chunking**: Shows students why context windows matter in practice.
4. **Uses tiktoken**: Makes tokenization concrete and measurable.
5. **Map-reduce summarization pattern**: Sophisticated approach that's directly relevant to real-world clinical AI.
6. **Terminal UI (Textual)**: Progressive display of results helps students understand the pipeline.

### 5.2 Gaps to Address in Restructuring

1. **No explicit concept teaching before the exercise**: Students jump into code without understanding what LLMs are.
2. **Only one exercise type**: Summarization is excellent but covers only one AI+FHIR pattern.
3. **No discussion of hallucination/safety**: Critical for healthcare audiences.
4. **No prompt engineering exploration**: Students run fixed prompts rather than experimenting.
5. **No temperature/parameter exploration**: Missing a key teaching opportunity.
6. **No assessment component**: No way to evaluate student understanding.
7. **Limited documentation**: The `.docx` file (LM Studio setup) doesn't appear to cover AI concepts.

### 5.3 Recommended Changes

1. Add a conceptual introduction phase before hands-on work
2. Add prompt engineering exercises (modify prompts, compare outputs)
3. Add at least one additional exercise type (NL-to-FHIR query recommended)
4. Add a hallucination/safety discussion with examples
5. Add temperature comparison demonstrations
6. Add a brief assessment or reflection activity
7. Create a student handout with key concepts, analogies, and resources

## 6. Additional Research Notes

### 6.1 FHIR Capabilities Relevant to AI Integration

FHIR provides several features that enable AI integration:
- **RESTful API + Search**: Programmatic access to health data enabling AI data retrieval
- **Multiple serialization formats**: XML, JSON, and RDF for flexible AI consumption
- **Structured resource taxonomy**: Administration, Clinical, Diagnostics, Medications, Financial categories
- **$everything operation**: Retrieves complete patient data in one call
- **CapabilityStatement**: AI systems can discover server capabilities programmatically
- Source: HL7 FHIR, https://www.hl7.org/fhir/, accessed 2026-10-07.

### 6.2 CDS Hooks for AI-Powered Decision Support

The CDS Hooks standard is directly relevant to AI+FHIR exercises:
- Hook-based pattern triggers CDS at specific clinical workflow points
- Uses FHIR as its data model
- Supports prefetch templates (FHIR queries declared by CDS services)
- Returns Cards with recommendations, suggestions, and links
- Feedback mechanism enables learning from clinician acceptance/rejection
- Source: CDS Hooks v2.0.1, https://cds-hooks.hl7.org/, accessed 2026-10-07.

### 6.3 RAG Architecture for Healthcare

RAG is particularly relevant for healthcare AI because:
1. Clinical knowledge changes rapidly (guidelines, drug approvals)
2. Patient-specific data must ground AI responses
3. Institutional policies vary between organizations
4. Traceability/auditability requires knowing which sources informed a response

The RAG pipeline involves: chunking documents, creating embeddings, storing in vector databases, retrieving relevant chunks at query time, and augmenting prompts with retrieved context. Traditional RAG can "destroy context" when chunking -- Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval addresses this by prepending explanatory context to each chunk before embedding, reducing retrieval failures by up to 67%.
- Sources: AWS (https://aws.amazon.com/what-is/retrieval-augmented-generation/), Anthropic (https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval), LangChain (https://www.langchain.com/blog/tutorial-chatgpt-over-your-data), all accessed 2026-10-07.
